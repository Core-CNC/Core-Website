Five signs your CNC tooling strategy is costing you more than it should

12 August 2026

Tooling spend rarely shows up as one clear number on a spreadsheet. It's spread across invoices, downtime, scrap, and the time someone spends chasing the right insert. Here are five signs it's costing more than it needs to.

  1. Tools break or chip with no obvious cause. Chipped edges and snapped inserts that don't line up with a hard material or an obvious crash are usually a sign the tooling isn't matched to the job, not bad luck.
  2. The same tool gives different results from batch to batch. If part quality drifts between runs using what's supposedly the same setup, the tooling, or how it's held, is rarely as consistent as the paperwork suggests.
  3. Tools get pushed well past their sensible life. Running an insert or drill past the point it should be changed, to avoid a changeover, usually costs more in scrap and rework than the changeover would have.
  4. One tooling range covers every job. Using the same general-purpose tooling across very different materials and jobs is convenient, but it's rarely the cheapest way to machine any one of them.
  5. No one's actually costed tooling per part. Most shops know what tooling costs on the invoice. Fewer know what it costs per part once tool life, cycle time and scrap are factored in. That number is usually the more useful one.

None of these are single fixes. But they're worth an honest look, because tooling is one of the few costs in a CNC operation you can actually change without buying new machines.
